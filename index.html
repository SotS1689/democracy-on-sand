    Part 3 · Elections already over

    Most House seats are settled before Election Day

    Most House districts are drawn so safely for one party that the November election is a formality. The real choice is made in that party's primary, and very few people vote in it.

    Part 4 · Whose opinion counts

    What most people want barely moves policy

    Political scientists Martin Gilens (Princeton) and Benjamin Page (Northwestern) looked at 1,779 proposed policy changes from 1981 to 2002. For each one they knew what average Americans (middle income) wanted, what affluent Americans (top 10% by income) wanted, and where organized interest groups stood, and whether the change became law.

    Sources and notes

    • Presidential results and turnout: U.S. election data compiled in USA Election Data.xlsx. “Share of voting-age adults” = turnout rate × winner's share of votes cast.
    • Before about 1828, many state legislatures chose presidential electors without a popular vote, so the early turnout figures are very low.
    • Congressional reelection rates: OpenSecrets. Approval: Gallup, averaged over each calendar year's polls (the thin line shows every poll).
    • The “r” number is a standard correlation: 0 means no relationship, 1 means the two move in lockstep.
    • Popular-vote losers: share of votes cast, from Wikipedia's list. In 1824 no one won a majority of electoral votes and the House of Representatives chose Adams.
    • Trust in government: Pew Research Center, “Public Trust in Government: 1958–2025” (data from Pew, the National Election Studies, Gallup, ABC/Washington Post, CBS/New York Times and CNN). The trend line is Pew's three-survey moving average.
    • Primaries: Unite America Institute, “Primary Problem”. A seat counts as decided in the primary when it is safe for one party in November. The voter share is of all voting-age Americans.
    • House margins: Ballotpedia, House races only.
    • Policy influence: Martin Gilens and Benjamin I. Page, “Testing Theories of American Politics,” Perspectives on Politics 12, no. 3 (2014): 564–81. Adoption rates are the paper's stated predicted probabilities with the other groups held at neutral. Coefficients are from Table 3, Model 4.
